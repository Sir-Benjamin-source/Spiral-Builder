# Paper #2: False-Positive Psychology (2011)

**Title:** False-Positive Psychology: Undisclosed Flexibility in Data Collection and Analysis Allows Presenting Anything as Significant  
**Authors:** Joseph P. Simmons, Leif D. Nelson, Uri Simonsohn  
**Venue:** Psychological Science  
**Why Selected:** Highly influential metascience paper that played a major role in exposing questionable research practices and helping launch broader awareness of the replication crisis. Excellent test case for assumption load, researcher degrees of freedom, and bullshit risk in the scientific process itself.

## Core Claims

- Researchers have significant "degrees of freedom" in data collection and analysis that are rarely disclosed.
- These flexibilities (optional stopping, selective reporting, outlier exclusion, etc.) dramatically increase the false-positive rate.
- It is possible to produce statistically significant results supporting almost any hypothesis through undisclosed analytical flexibility.
- Greater transparency and disclosure standards are needed to reduce false positives in psychological science.

## Grandma’s Wisdom + Grokulator Evaluation

- **Evidence Strength:** High. The paper combines simulation work with concrete demonstrations of how common practices inflate false positives. The core argument is logically tight and has been widely supported by subsequent metascience research.
- **Bullshit Risk:** Low to Moderate. The claims are scoped appropriately to methodological critique rather than overgeneralized prescriptions, though the dramatic phrasing ("presenting anything as significant") carries some rhetorical risk if taken too literally.
- **Assumption Load:** Moderate. Key assumptions include that undisclosed flexibility is widespread, that the simulated scenarios reflect real researcher behavior, and that increased transparency will meaningfully reduce false positives without major unintended consequences.
- **Discordance Flags:** Moderate. While the paper itself was well-received in metascience circles, it highlighted a major discordance between the public image of rigorous psychological science and actual common research practices.
- **Other Observations:** Strong coherence and clear logical structure. The paper effectively uses simulation as a form of adversarial testing against common practices.

## Comparison to Conventional Review

Traditional peer review accepted the paper, and it gained significant traction in the emerging metascience community. However, many working researchers initially resisted or downplayed its implications. Conventional processes were relatively slow to translate its critique into widespread changes in practice and incentives. Our approach would have more explicitly quantified the assumption load around "researcher degrees of freedom" and flagged the systemic discordance between claimed rigor and actual flexibility earlier and more systematically.

## Strengths (under our lens)

- Clear, falsifiable demonstration of how common practices produce false positives.
- Strong adversarial testing via simulation.
- Directly addresses a core source of bullshit in the scientific literature.

## Gaps / Risks

- Assumes that increased disclosure/transparency will be sufficient without deeper incentive changes.
- Does not deeply model the social and career pressures that drive questionable practices.
- Limited discussion of how these issues manifest differently across subfields or research cultures.

## Corpus Takeaway

This paper stands as one of the clearer and more honest examinations of how scientific claims can be manufactured through flexible but undisclosed practices. Our framework highlights both its logical strength and the remaining assumption gaps around whether transparency reforms alone can address the deeper incentive structures that sustain those practices. It serves as a strong positive example of internal scientific self-critique that aligns well with Grandma-style demands for evidence over institutional reputation.
